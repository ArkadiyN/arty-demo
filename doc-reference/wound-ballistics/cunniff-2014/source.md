### A Method to Describe the Statistical Aspects of Armor Penetration, Human Vulnerability and Lethality due to Fragmenting Munitions

Philip M. Cunniff
.

A dimensionless areal density parameter is introduced as the principal independent penetration variable for estimation of ballistic limit of an armor system. Published metrology data for munition fragments shows that a probability density function can be fit to fragment mass per unit presented area data, independent of mass. The expected variation in presented area of a projectile with a fixed mass is shown to be function of the mean area for all projectiles within certain naturally fragmenting munitions. Data for environmental drag, gelatin penetration, armor penetration, and penetration of witness materials is analyzed in light of the expected variability in dimensionless areal density for actual munition fragments. The analysis suggests that a statistical approach to these processes is required, since ballistics limits can easily double or more over the mean and can easily be cut in half or less, compared to the mean, depending ONLY on striking attitude of the penetrator. Using a statistical approach, the problem of determining the functional utility of armor materials is reformatted from a coarse stochastic approximation to a discrete Bayesian statistical analysis.

### INTRODUCTION

Flexible body armor materials are intended to provide protection primarily against fragmenting munitions. These munitions may be naturally fragmenting or may contain pre-formed fragments, or both. Body armor systems must be capable of providing protection against a broad spectrum of these fragmenting threats. A methodology to estimate the effectiveness of that protection that includes definitions of key munition variables (such as initial velocity, fragment mass, number of fragments, and fragment shape), and defines the effect of transformative processes (such as velocity reduction due to air drag, and velocity reduction due to armor impact or tissue penetration and wound track creation), and ultimately leads to assessment of injury is sorely needed. A crude ranking of the effectiveness of fragmentation protective armor systems can be obtained by comparison of the ballistic limits of candidate armor systems to the ballistic limits of an existing or a fielded item against some arbitrary set of projectiles. The risk of this approach is obvious. Sullivan and Halstead [1], for example, discuss an approach intended to “allow the client to analyze a large quantity of data to determine performance within the zone of mixed results”. Data for the penetration of armor under impact by fragment simulators is analyzed in the range of striking velocities between the low complete striking velocity and the high partial striking velocity. This approach, of course, fails to relate the performance of the armor to actual munitions, and similarly fails to relate the variability of the armor to real projectiles. These projectiles were found to be inadequate for that purpose for several reasons, including poor quality control of the bullet (leading to large variability in test results) and the high deformation of the projectile observed after ballistic testing of personnel armor compared to the deformation of steel or cast iron projectiles. Handgun bullets are simply not representative of munition fragments. Ballistic impact testing is facilitated by the use of relatively low-cost high tolerance fragment simulators, but fragment simulators are of little use except to the extent that they can be used to predict the penetration performance of actual munition fragments. Even if a set of fragment simulators can be used to approximate real munition fragments, it remains to understand the significance of stopping one set of fragments while failing to stop another. Examination of the challenge presented by fragmenting munitions clearly reveals that optimal armor is not necessarily impenetrable armor simply because impenetrable armor is so unwieldy. Once the inevitability of less than impenetrable armor is accepted, metrics for armor performance become complicated; morbidity and mortality are merely mitigated, usually for the least injurious threats. The benefits of less than ideal armor (or any other armor) are offset to a certain extent by deleterious effects, including increased fatigue, heat stress, encumbrance and reduction of mobility. To understand the trade-off even between equal weight and encumbrance systems, details of the threat are required as part of the analysis and the analysis can only be realistically performed on real-world threats that are anticipated to be encountered in battle. A definition of armor performance that is limited to specification of ballistic limits invariably requires that the ballistic limit velocities of small fragments from exploding munitions be greater than the ballistic limit velocities of larger fragments; that’s the opposite of optimal for fragmentation protective armor. A complete understanding of the dilemma requires knowledge of explosives and fracture mechanics but the basic facts can be appreciated from a few photographs of exploding munitions. Such images reveal that following detonation, munitions appear to expand uniformly. That uniform expansion implies uniform velocity of the fragments that are being expelled from the munition. That definition of armor performance is revealed to be the exact reverse of what it should be since we know the initial velocity of all fragments from these munitions is approximately the same at the time of munition burst and we know that the velocity of small fragments is reduced more quickly that the velocity of larger ones under the action air drag, this knowledge leads to the certainty that large fragments arrive at a target first and at a higher velocity than smaller ones, at least on average, as we shall discuss below. The most injurious threats from naturally fragmenting munitions like mortars, howitzers, rockets and certain classes of grenades is from large fragments traveling at higher velocities than small fragments. Although it may be expedient to specify equivalence to existing systems it’s more appropriate to describe the actual threat and set requirements based on what’s possible to do to mitigate that threat. That’s difficult. However, this approach at least affords the possibility to encourage progress in the direction of the best possible performance. We need to understand what can be specified, what is required, what part of a requirement must be waived, and to afford a means to allow for incremental improvement in the direction of enhanced system performance. The way to encourage that incremental improvement in the direction of enhanced performance is to model the armor in the use condition, under impact by actual munition fragments. During World War II, a series of “fragment simulating” projectiles was developed by the U.S. Army Watertown Arsenal Laboratory for test and evaluation of aircrew body armor [5-10]. During the 1940’s and 1950’s the U.S. Army conducted extensive wound ballistics evaluations. These tests were conducted using standardized projectiles (cubes and spheres). Later, in the 1960’s work to define so- called “serious” and “lethal” wounds was undertaken [11]. Those developments, which led to methods to evaluate the probability of incapacitation given a hit were

augmented by analyses of environmental drag and armor penetration [12] to form the basis for present-day casualty assessment analysis. The development of “Casualty Reduction Analysis” computer codes followed those results [13,14]. By the time Kevlar armor was being evaluated, well established procedures had already been developed to help assess armor performance. The decision to maintain the weight of the ballistic protective vest and helmet were not ad hoc; they were guided by careful analysis. The computer programs of the early 1970’s required a considerable amount of simplification to accommodate the relatively low computational power of computers at that time.

When fragments strike an armor system (or the human body), penetration depends on the mass and on the so-called “projected area” of the projectile. Projected area (used interchangeably with “presented area” here) can be thought of as the footprint of the projectile at the time of strike; it’s a bit more correct to think of projected area as the shadow of the projectile from a light source placed at the point of detonation of the munition. The mass of the projectile and the presented area are equivalent in importance in predicting penetration of the armor.
As part of prior work in this area [16], a striking and residual velocity database (including V50 data) for fragmentation protective composite and fabric armor consisting of over 10,000 shots on target has been compiled and will be briefly synopsized here. Projectile types represented in the data base include steel and

tungsten right circular cylinders, “chisel-nosed” steel fragment simulators, steel cubes, and steel and tungsten spheres. The projectile mass range represented in the data base is from 1- to 128-grains. Striking obliquities of 0-, 15-, 30-, 45-, and 60- degrees were available for some of the data sets. The model yields acceptable results for a large number of armor materials under impact by a large number of projectiles. A plot of the AdAp/mp model for Kevlar KM2 fabric is provided in Figure 1 to illustrate the efficacy of the AdAp/mp model for relatively large projectiles. It should be emphasized that the “model” refereed to here is an extraordinarily simple (usually polynomial) fit to the experimental data that (certain heuristics aside) predicts observed impact response within experimental error. That heuristics are required to fill gaps in the description of penetration should not necessarily be understood to be a problem. Ad hoc patches are acceptable in a utilitarian sense, failing a more complete understanding of the armor or munition, or of human vulnerability response. The failure of the AdAp/mp model to predict small spheres is highlighted here to bring that point home. It is potentially possible to modify the model at a more fundamental step, but that’s not attempted. It is important that we not use models that are too restrictive as part of a Bayesian prior belief system; in some respects, ad hoc models that approximate the actual physics at hand, even if the model parameters don’t correspond to physical entities, are entirely appropriate here.

### FRAGMENT VELOCITY

When a munition detonates, fragments are expelled from the munition at approximately a constant velocity [35, 36]. For a complex-shape munition, the velocity may be a predictable function of position around the munition, but is usually thought to be constant initially in the radial direction. A more refined study of the nucleation, growth, and coalescence of the microscopic voids and cracks reveals that larger fragments are propelled by the detonation for a slightly longer period of time than smaller fragments (because of gas dynamics and geometry) and therefore have a somewhat higher initial velocity. A precise definition of the physics contributing to setting projectile velocity following munition detonation isn’t necessary for the evaluation of either real or notional armor systems challenged by existing munitions so long as the munitions are available and can be tested and measured experimentally, or can be accurately modeled in some respects. It’s really only important for this discussion that the velocity be something we can estimate and something we can measure in live fire. The experimental characterization of warhead fragment data is conducted on individual weapons to determine the velocity, spatial frequency (number of fragments per unit solid angle increment), weight and shape of fragments as a function of conical region around the munition center-line. The main discovery of this work is that the probability density function for presented area per unit mass of munition fragments is a smooth well behaved function. That function is frequently well approximated as a Gaussian distribution (but not always), and most significantly, that function is independent of mass. That means a single probability density function for projectile areal density can be used for all of the projectiles from a munition. The normalized probability density function for projectile presented area per unit mass of several munitions is shown in Figure 3. Knowing a probability density function for projectile areal density allows for estimation of penetration in a statistical sense. That is, for a fixed target areal density, and a fixed projectile mass, the probability density function for projectile areal density will indicate the range of projectile areal densities that the projectile can take on. Inspection of the probability density functions presented in Figure 3 shows that it’s possible for the presented area to have a wide distribution (from as low as 20% of the mean up to 200% of the mean). This knowledge is essential in predicting the velocity reduction due to environmental drag for stable fragments where drag force is directly proportionate to instantaneous presented area, it’s obviously essential in predicting penetration of armor as illustrated in the V50 plot of Figure 1 above, and it’s essential for predicting depth of penetration in other extreme dynamic events like tissue penetration. Remarkably, for the purpose of the estimation of projected area as used in the environmental drag, in tissue penetration and in armor resistance to penetration, a single probability density function for projectile projected area, independent of projectile mass can frequently be estimated.

### VELOCITY REDUCTION DUE TO AIR DRAG

For the purpose of measuring air drag resistance, an assumption (so-called “uniform orientation” assumption) is frequently made that fragments tumble when accelerated from the warhead. Over a sufficiently large distance (and time), the drag force based on the mean presented area of a fragment is expected to represent the average response. The key assumption is that fragments take on a sufficiently large number of orientations over key timescales (lengthscales) of the event. That means, for example, if coefficient of drag varies with Mach number, projectiles should reorient sufficiently during the time it takes to go from one Mach regime to another to cover the range between the extremes of the shape distribution with equal likelihood. It is frequently reported that G.I. Taylor remarked in 1940 that rectangular blocks, when fired from guns, would probably rotate so that all aspects of presented area relative to the flight path would occur with about equal frequency.

### MUNITION CHARACTERIZATION

The objectives of a functional utility assessment of fragmentation protective body armor systems are straightforward, if not simple; the assessment seeks to measure the effectiveness of the armor under conditions that may be encountered in use, namely to measure the relative change in the ability of the users of the armor to function (either in terms of their ability to perform certain functions or in terms of reduced morbidity and mortality) when challenged by a range of fragmenting munitions. The process of performing the assessment is complex in part because of the complexity of the events that comprise the problem. Munitions detonate and expel fragments of differing mass and shape and having differing spatial and velocity distributions. These may be functions of the material properties and production process of the munition, and they may be related to the explosive type and conditions of burst. The main events that comprise the problem are detonation of the munition, fly- out of fragments under the action of environmental drag, terminal ballistic impact onto armor or materials, and ballistic impact onto human tissue. Detonation of the munition results in mass, number, velocity, and shape distributions of fragments. These distributions may differ with position around the munition. The burst characteristics of many real fragmenting munitions are assumed to be symmetric within a solid angle measured from the nose of the projectile, but the mass and velocity characteristics of the fragments that are expelled from a munition are assumed to differ within different solid angles; we suggest discrete regions of the munition be modeled as separate munitions. The action of environmental drag during the fly-out phase of the event may be thought of as a transformation of the initial velocity distribution. Drag, for example, tends to reduce the velocity of fragments in proportion to the instantaneous presented area of the fragment perpendicular to the velocity vector during flight. Experimental data available for the fragmenting characteristics of munitions are collected during so-called static burst tests. In a static burst test, the munition is exploded and data concerning the velocity, mass, and distribution density of the fragments are recorded. The data give the velocity and density of fragments (in number of fragments / steradian) for each of a number of fragment masses as a function of a solid angle from the nose of the munition. Results of these tests are expressed assuming the munition may be approximated by a point burst.

In munitions tables, velocity is expressed at the boundaries of the spray zones and may be considered a continuous variable independent of projectile mass, density, and geometry. The tabulated fragmentation data provide the average mass of fragments within a weight class observed during the experimental tests. They do not imply a continuous distribution of projectile mass; in fact, discrete mass distributions are assumed. Projectile depth of penetration into collection medium is an indication of striking velocity and mass per unit presented area (for normal impact obliquity). For stable projectile penetration, the striking orientation is sufficient to predict depth, or, alternately, for a known effective presented area and mass, depth is sufficient to predict velocity. For projectiles that re-orient during penetration, that’s not the case. Re-orientation of projectiles due to unequally distributed pressure over the strike-face of the fragment may tend to cause presented area to effectively increase and to cause the probability density function to become effectively multi-modal. That analysis is beyond the scope of this paper.

### ORIENTATION OF TARGET RELATIVE TO MUNITION BURST POINT

For a munition with fully symmetrical burst characteristics, the probability of a projectile striking a given target (a human phantom, in this case) is a function of the number of fragments emitted from the munition, the square of the distance from the munition burst point to the target, and the total area and attitude of the target relative to the munition. In this work, with the human phantom centered at the origin of the space, we assign munition burst location to be a series of equally spaced points distributed over concentric spheres [60-62], as illustrated in Figure 7. Each of these points in space is considered a “viewpoint” onto the phantom. Rather than have the points at equal azimuth and elevation angles, they are distributed evenly at equal radial distance from the intersection of the anatomical planes of the body. Impact points (corresponding to computational elements on the body of the phantom) are evaluated for penetration if the dot product of the outward normal vector to the element and the viewpoint vector are numerically greater than zero (points on the side of the phantom that would correspond to “exit wounds” are not evaluated for penetration). For munitions that have asymmetric burst patterns, the munition is re-oriented at the burst point so that each spray zone illuminates the target.

The problem of determining functional utility of a body armor system requires a definition of the threat; in this work, we have assumed that definition of that function will take the form of the definition of a joint probability density function for probability of initial fragment velocity, mass number and shape for each of a set of spray zones defined by solid angles about the munition center line. For preformed-fragment or naturally fragmenting munitions comprised of a single material and having a symmetric burst pattern the munition itself may be modeled as a single item. The more general definition of a munition treats each spray zone as a separate threat with an associated probability of engagement. In that way, for example, each of the spray zones of a howitzer may be assigned some probability, based on geometry and expected relationship of the orientation of the munition to the target. The relative probabilities of encountering other munitions may be assigned based on Ground Intelligence, and will assign a relative weight to the probability of encountering each munition type (and consequently, each spray zone therein). For a mix of munition types comprised of, for example 10 different munitions, each having 25 spray zones, that's 250 modeled threats, each assigned some fraction of the assumed 100% probability of encountering a munition. The initial definition of fragment velocity and mass probability density functions (prior belief) are best approximated from high resolution finite element simulations of bursting munitions. The notion that small fragments have sufficiently similar shape to larger ones, at least for the purpose of determining the probability density function for normalized presented area is not inconsistent with a mechanics-based stress analysis of the munitions from which the fragments were formed. Following the detonation of the explosive, material in the wall of the munition expands and stress is propagated through the body. Planes of high stress are created as a results of the munition geometry and to the forcing function of the expanding explosive. Fracture occurs on those high stress planes, nearly simultaneously. Complex unloading and re-loading of mechanical stresses occurs; it is perhaps not so unlikely that fracture occurs on parallel planes at differing length scales. As insightful as such an explanation might be, it is not necessary for the purpose of evaluating munition performance. To the extent that a theoretical development limits the ability to fit experimental data, the theory may in fact be a detriment. Fragment shape, therefore, being influenced by highly complex fracture mechanics, typically not well modeled by finite element simulations, may be more appropriately characterized through traditional metrology; specifically through the use of an icosahedral gauge. An icosahedron is comprised of 20 equilateral triangles arranged such that each of the 12 vertexes of the icosahedron corresponds to the vertices of 5 triangles. The vertices of the icosahedron are located at the corners of three orthogonal Golden Rectangles. For an icosahedron of edge length 1, the coordinates of the rectangles are (0, ±φ/2, ±1/2), (±1/2, 0, ±φ/2), (±φ/2, ±1/2, 0), with . Viewpoints onto the fragment are made normal to the centroid of 5 triangles containing a common vertex, additional viewpoints are taken at the 6 common vertices of those triangles (these vertices correspond to the

centroids of the uppermost 6 pentagons of a dodecahedron, and the vertices of the icosahedrons correspond to the centroids of the pentagons of the dodecahedron, since the dodecahedron is the dual-polyhedron of the icosahedron) additional viewpoints are taken normal to the centroid of the five additional triangles having a common side with the original 5. The 16 viewpoints represent equally spaced viewpoints from one hemisphere of a spherical coordinate system, these viewpoints are each at equal radial distance from the origin of the icosahedron. Further, the utility problem requires definition of the transformative processes of fragment fly-out, armor engagement, and tissue penetration. The fly-out and armor engagement problems includes a velocity transformation that may reduce velocity to zero (such as the action of air drag on small fragments or partial penetration of armor). The tissue penetration transformation results in a penetration trajectory distribution function, including depth of penetration and tissue type penetrated for each cell on the surface of a human phantom.

### SUMMARY

The discovery that a single probability density function for fragment mass per unit presented area can be developed to describe the statistical nature of mass per unit presented area for munition fragments (particularly those expelled from naturally fragmenting munitions, but not exclusively limited to those), has allowed for the heretofore coarse, stochastic problem of lethality analysis (or munition effectiveness or analysis of the safe distance from a bursting munition) to be rephrased as a discrete statistical analysis. Further, with that statistical analysis, for the first time, we can now place confidence limits on our estimations, and continuously refine our prior knowledge. The variance encountered by the use of mean presented area in the calculation of velocity reduction due to air drag, armor penetration, and tissue penetration have been shown to be considerable. The method proposed to assess armor performance does not reduce that variance; rather, it attempts to quantify the statistical significance of the variance. In addition to applications previously discussed here, work to define fragmentation hazards, and safe distances from exploding munitions [65-67] should benefit from this learning \[. A procedure for the analysis of the functional utility of fragmentation protective armor that includes a statistical description of a munition in terms of a multi-variate probability density function for initial fragment velocity, mass, shape and numerical density of fragments (flux of fragments) has been discussed. This probability density function is assumed to be defined both in terms of experimental results available from data collection from the detonation of actual munitions, and may be further defined by analytical or numerical results. This definition of the munition is acted upon by transformative events, including environmental drag, armor engagement and tissue penetration which tend to attenuate velocity. The tissue penetration process is said to transform the initial probability density function into one expressed in terms of wound tracts associated with surface elements on a human phantom, type and extent of tissue damage, and ultimately leads to an assessment of injury.
